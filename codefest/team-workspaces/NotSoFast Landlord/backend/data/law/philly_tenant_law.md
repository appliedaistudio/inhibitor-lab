## Self-help eviction is illegal

**Citation:** 68 P.S. § 250.501 (PA Landlord-Tenant Act of 1951, Article V)

In Pennsylvania, a landlord **cannot** remove a tenant, change locks, shut off
utilities, or remove a tenant's belongings without going through the formal
court eviction process. This is called a "self-help" eviction and it is
**illegal**, even if the tenant is behind on rent.

The only lawful way to remove a tenant is:
1. Landlord files a complaint in ejectment with the Municipal Court
2. Tenant receives notice and has a chance to respond
3. A judge holds a hearing
4. If the judge rules for the landlord, the judge issues an Order for
   Possession
5. A constable or sheriff carries out the eviction — not the landlord

If your landlord locks you out, removes your belongings, or shuts off your
utilities to force you to leave, that is a crime under PA law. Call 911
immediately and contact the Philly Tenant Hotline at 267-443-2500.

## Notice before eviction

**Citation:** 68 P.S. § 250.501(b); Philadelphia Code § 9-804 (Good Cause Eviction)

Before a landlord can file an eviction complaint, they must give the tenant
written notice. The required notice period depends on the reason for the
eviction and whether the tenancy is covered by Philadelphia's Good Cause
Eviction ordinance:

- **Nonpayment of rent:** At least 10 days' written notice
- **End of lease term / termination for cause under PA state law:** At least
  15 days for tenancies of less than one year; 30 days for tenancies of one
  year or more
- **Good Cause Eviction (Philadelphia):** Landlords of covered units must
  have "good cause" to terminate a tenancy. Good cause includes nonpayment
  of rent, material lease violations, and certain owner-occupancy reasons.
  No-cause terminations are generally prohibited for covered tenancies
  (Phila. Code § 9-804).

A notice that does not comply with these requirements can be challenged in
court. The landlord also cannot skip this step and proceed straight to
filing — the notice must be served first.

## Constructive eviction and habitability

**Citation:** Pugh v. Holmes, 486 Pa. 272 (1979) (implied warranty of habitability); PA Municipalities Planning Code; Philadelphia Property Maintenance Code (PM-605 et seq.)

Every residential lease in Pennsylvania includes an **implied warranty of
habitability**. This means the landlord must deliver and maintain the
premises in a condition fit for human habitation. Required conditions include:

- **Heat:** During cold months (typically Oct 1 – Apr 30 per Philadelphia PM
  Code), the landlord must provide working heat capable of maintaining the
  unit at a minimum temperature.
- **Running water and hot water**
- **Working plumbing, electricity, and sanitary conditions**
- **Structural integrity** — no dangerous defects
- **Freedom from vermin, rodent, and pest infestations**
- **Smoke detectors and carbon monoxide detectors** where required
- **Compliance with lead paint disclosure and abatement rules** for units
  with children under 7

If the landlord fails to maintain these conditions after written notice and
reasonable time to repair, the tenant may have several remedies, including:

- Reporting the violation to Philadelphia Licenses and Inspections (L&I)
- Withholding rent in an escrow account (only with proper legal procedure)
- Repair and deduct (limited circumstances; consult an attorney first)
- Terminating the lease and moving out (constructive eviction)
- Suing the landlord for damages

⚠️ **Withholding rent is a technical legal step with strict procedural
requirements.** A tenant who stops paying rent without following the proper
escrow procedure can still be evicted. Always consult Community Legal
Services (215-981-3700) before withholding rent.

## Retaliation is prohibited

**Citation:** 68 P.S. § 250.205; Philadelphia Fair Housing Ordinance § 9-804(11)

A landlord cannot retaliate against a tenant for exercising legal rights.
Retaliation includes raising the rent, reducing services, terminating the
lease, or filing an eviction action because the tenant:

- Filed a complaint with Licenses and Inspections (L&I)
- Reported a housing code violation
- Joined or organized a tenant association
- Testified in court or at a government agency
- Requested a repair in writing

If a tenant faces an adverse action within a short window after engaging in
a protected activity, the landlord has the burden to prove the action was
not retaliatory. Report retaliation to the Philadelphia Fair Housing
Commission or Community Legal Services.

## Security deposits

**Citation:** 68 P.S. § 250.511a, § 250.511b, § 250.512

Under Pennsylvania law:

- **Maximum amount:** During the first year of a tenancy, a landlord may
  not collect more than **two months' rent** as a security deposit. After
  the first year, the maximum drops to **one month's rent**.
- **Escrow account:** Security deposits over $100 must be held in an
  escrow account in a PA financial institution, and the tenant must be
  notified in writing of the location.
- **Return deadline:** The landlord must return the deposit (minus
  itemized deductions for actual damages) within **30 days** after the
  tenant moves out and provides a forwarding address.
- **Penalty for failure to return:** If the landlord fails to return the
  deposit or provide an itemized list of deductions within 30 days, the
  tenant may sue for **double the amount wrongfully withheld**, plus
  court costs and attorney's fees.

Normal wear and tear is not a valid deduction. Cleaning charges beyond
reasonable cleaning, pre-existing damage, and painting normally used units
are also typically not valid.

## Landlord entry

**Citation:** 68 P.S. § 250.501 (implied); common law

Pennsylvania state law does not set a specific notice period for landlord
entry, but the lease usually governs. In the absence of lease language, the
landlord must give **reasonable notice** (generally 24 hours) and may enter
only for legitimate purposes:

- Repairs and maintenance
- Inspections with notice
- Showing the unit to prospective tenants or buyers
- Emergencies (no notice required)

A landlord who repeatedly enters without notice or for harassment purposes
may be violating the tenant's right to quiet enjoyment and may be subject
to a Fair Housing Commission complaint.

## Discrimination is illegal

**Citation:** Fair Housing Act (42 U.S.C. § 3601 et seq.); Philadelphia Fair Practices Ordinance (Phila. Code § 9-1101 et seq.)

Under federal, state, and Philadelphia law, it is illegal for a landlord
to discriminate against a tenant or applicant based on:

- Race, color, national origin, ancestry
- Religion
- Sex, gender identity, sexual orientation
- Familial status (having children)
- Disability (including requiring reasonable accommodations for disabilities)
- Source of income (including housing vouchers / Section 8 — Philadelphia
  specifically protects voucher holders)
- Age

The Philadelphia Commission on Human Relations and the Philadelphia Fair
Housing Commission both accept complaints. The federal HUD office does as
well. A tenant who experiences discrimination should document the incident
in writing and contact Community Legal Services or the Legal Clinic for the
Disabled.

Reasonable accommodations — including permission for emotional support
animals and service animals — cannot be denied solely because of a "no
pets" policy.

## Illegal lockouts and utility shutoffs

**Citation:** 68 P.S. § 250.501; Philadelphia Code § 9-804(9)

If a landlord changes the locks, removes a tenant's belongings, or shuts
off electricity, gas, or water to force a tenant to leave, this is an
**illegal lockout**. It is a crime in Pennsylvania.

Immediate steps:

1. **Call 911.** Illegal lockout is a police matter.
2. **Call the Philly Tenant Hotline at 267-443-2500.**
3. **Do not confront the landlord alone.** Bring a witness or law enforcement.
4. **Document everything:** photos, texts, timestamps, witness statements.
5. **Contact Community Legal Services (215-981-3700)** the same day.

Tenants can file in Municipal Court for restoration of possession and
damages. The Fair Housing Commission also handles illegal lockout complaints.

## Free legal help for Philadelphia renters

Philadelphia has one of the strongest tenant legal aid networks in the
country. All resources below are free for qualifying low-income tenants.

- **Community Legal Services (CLS):** 215-981-3700 — free legal representation
  for low-income Philadelphians facing eviction, lockouts, utility shutoffs,
  and landlord disputes. Offices at 1424 Chestnut St (Center City) and 1410
  W. Erie Ave (North Philadelphia).
- **Philly Tenant Hotline:** 267-443-2500 — staffed by the Tenant Union
  Representative Network (TURN). Interpretation services available.
- **Philadelphia Eviction Prevention Project (PEPP):** phillytenant.org —
  comprehensive tenant resources and Know Your Rights guides.
- **Philadelphia Right to Counsel program:** guarantees free legal
  representation to eligible low-income renters facing eviction in covered
  ZIP codes (19121, 19124, 19131, 19132, 19134, 19139, 19141, 19144, 19153,
  19154). Immigration status does not affect eligibility.
- **Philadelphia Eviction Diversion Program:** eviction-diversion.phila.gov —
  mediation and rental assistance to resolve disputes before they reach
  court.
- **Legal Clinic for the Disabled:** assistance for tenants with disabilities.
- **SeniorLAW Center:** assistance for tenants aged 60+.
- **Philadelphia Commission on Human Relations / Fair Housing Commission:**
  handles housing discrimination complaints.

## If you are in immediate danger

- **Call 911** — illegal lockouts, violent landlord behavior, or immediate
  threats to safety are criminal matters.
- **Philly Tenant Hotline 267-443-2500** — staffed resource line.
- **National Domestic Violence Hotline: 1-800-799-7233** — if landlord
  behavior involves domestic violence or stalking patterns.
